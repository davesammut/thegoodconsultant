Notes from the field

The best workshop I ever ran failed

4 min read

I once ran a workshop to align a leadership team on a platform strategy. It ended in near silence. Two directors hadn't spoken to each other in twenty minutes. I left thinking I'd wasted everyone's afternoon.

Three months later, the company made the call that workshop was supposed to produce. Not despite what happened in the room. Because of it.

What a good workshop is actually for

I used to think my job was to get a room to agreement by the end of the session. A tidy set of decisions on a whiteboard, a plan everyone nodded at, a good feeling leaving the building.

Those workshops photograph well. They rarely change anything. The agreement in the room is often just the disagreement, postponed.

The disagreement was the point

What that difficult session actually did was make a real disagreement visible for the first time — one that had been quietly shaping every stalled decision for a year, without ever being said out loud in the same room by the two people who owned it.

Nobody could keep avoiding it once it was on the table. That was uncomfortable, and it was the only thing that moved the decision forward.

The lesson

A good workshop's job is not to reach agreement in the room. It's to make the disagreement visible enough that it can finally be resolved — usually later, usually somewhere else, and usually by the people who actually own the decision.

If a workshop feels smooth and everyone leaves satisfied, ask what wasn't said. That's often where the real work still is.
