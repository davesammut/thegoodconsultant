The book

The Good Consultant

Most consulting books begin when the contract is signed. This one begins much earlier — with a referral, a first conversation, a difficult question, a client who isn't quite sure what problem they're actually trying to solve.

The manuscript follows the complete lifecycle of a consulting relationship: from the referral and first conversation, through understanding what to promise, shaping and winning the work, joining the client, creating change, leaving well, and building the reputation that leads to whatever comes next. It answers one question: how do you become someone people trust with difficult technology decisions? Not through theory — through stories, observations, principles, approaches, and reflection.

It isn't a consulting handbook, a technology book, or a management book. Consulting is simply the environment where judgement is tested every day — the same judgement that creates confidence, navigates uncertainty, improves conversations, and leaves organisations stronger than they were found. It's equally for founders, CTOs, architects, investors, and board members as it is for consultants. Reading it now means watching it get written.

Why I'm writing this

After twenty-five years of consulting, I've realised that the most valuable lessons were never the frameworks, technologies or methodologies. They were the observations that quietly changed how I think. This book is my attempt to capture those observations while I'm still learning them myself.

Story → Observation → Principle → Approach → Reflection

Most chapters follow this rhythm. Frameworks — DDD, Domain Storytelling, Event Storming, Team Topologies, Wardley Mapping, AI — show up in the Approach, but only ever as supporting actors. They're never the main character.

A defining idea

Comfortable Being Uncomfortable

Good consultants aren't hired because they already know the answer. They're trusted because they know how to discover it. Consultants don't carry a collection of answers — they carry a toolbox for navigating uncertainty: Event Storming, Domain Storytelling, Wardley Mapping, Team Topologies, facilitation, architecture, observation, interviews, experiments, AI, pair programming. None of these are answers. They're ways of discovering better ones, and knowing the difference is one of the quieter skills this book keeps coming back to.

The hidden thread

Every stage of consulting introduces a healthy tension.

The book doesn't resolve these. It explores how experienced consultants navigate them — because judgement is the ability to hold a tension well, not eliminate it.

Promise ↔ Guarantee Confidence ↔ Certainty Curiosity ↔ Assumption Challenge ↔ Trust Speaking ↔ Listening Business ↔ Technology AI ↔ Human Judgement Delivery ↔ Legacy

Field Notes

Observations, not opinions.

Throughout the manuscript you'll find recurring Field Notes — short, standalone observations gathered across decades of consulting, and three years of deliberate pair programming with teams across different organisations. They're not tips, and they're not lessons. They're things I noticed about trust, leadership, behaviour, engineering culture, collaboration, and judgement, offered as observations rather than conclusions.

Field Note — from Why Good Developers Behave Badly

One of the strongest predictors of a successful pairing session wasn't technical ability. It was whether both people left believing they had contributed something and learned something.

The manuscript

A journey in six parts.

Not a finished table of contents — a living list, organised the way the book itself is organised: as the lifecycle of a consulting relationship, from the referral that starts it to the reputation that outlives it. Some chapters are a full draft, most are still notes and an intention.

Part I — Before the Contract

Great consulting begins before the proposal.

This part follows an opportunity from the first conversation through to shaping an engagement that both sides can responsibly commit to. It isn't about sales technique — it's about creating confidence without manufacturing certainty. How it ends here is also, in a sense, where the book ends: the reputation built by finishing one engagement well is usually why somebody starts the next conversation.
